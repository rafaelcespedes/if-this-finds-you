_2 November 2026. Working name so far: Seedhand. Engine measured at `e89dd3b` (main). Reference library read
at 484 entries. Trademark and domain checks were run today and will go stale; re-run them before you commit._

## Summary

- **Recommended name: Plumín** (ploo-MEEN). In Spanish it's the nib of a fountain pen, the part that touches the
  paper. It reads as "pen" across the Romance languages, has no trademark in software in the US register or in
  TMview, and `plumin.ink` is free.
- **Next best:** **Seedhand** (the cleanest legally, already in the code, but "seed" is AI-image vocabulary),
  **Penfolk** (warm and people-first, zero marks), **Everyhand** (says "inclusive" in one word, zero marks).
- **Ruled out after checking:** Fair Hand (a kids' handwriting app already uses it), Longhand (an app for
  handwritten notes plus live marks for direct mail and software), Otherhand (Cheetos released "The Other Hand"
  font), Tintero (a writing-studio app), Ductus and Rasgo (live marks in software classes), Penfriend (a typeface
  and an AI writer).
- **Strongest facts:** 8,589,934,592 possible writers (more than the 8.2 billion people alive); 0 identical
  letters in 47,094 chances on one page; sources spanning 1522 to 2024; about 4,000 to 5,000 letters drawn per
  second; 484 open references.
- **Two claims need to wait.** "Five centuries of handwriting" and "billions of *different-looking* hands" are true
  of the research and the maths, but the engine today still draws from one 1967 source and many hands look like
  siblings. Both change when the traced traditions and letter variety land. Until then, use the "today" versions
  marked below.

## 1. Naming

### 1.1 What the name has to do

1. Feel warm and made by hand, like the product.
2. Point at writing: a pen, a hand, paper, a writer.
3. Work across languages, since the hands come from many countries.
4. Be clear of existing handwriting apps, fonts and software trademarks.
5. Avoid sounding like AI. The product's first promise is that there's no AI model inside.
6. Have a domain path: an exact `.com` is rare now, so `.ink`, `.co`, `.app` or a short prefix count.
7. Respect Rafael's other names (Rueca, the mini series, Sombra, Trama, Matiz, Molde, Vaivén) without reusing them.

### 1.5 Recommendation: Plumín

1. **It names the exact spot where handwriting happens.** The nib is where ink leaves the pen. Every letter this
   tool draws is a simulated nib moving across paper.
2. **It's warm and small,** like the product's best moments: a note, a label, a letter.
3. **It travels.** Spanish, French, Catalan and Portuguese speakers hear "pen" or "feather" in it; English and Dutch
   speakers hear "plume". It's easy to say once you've heard it.
4. **It's clear where it matters.** No mark in software in the US register or TMview; the exact marks that exist
   sit in lighting, clothing and cosmetics.
5. **`plumin.ink` is free,** a domain that says ink. `plumin.com` shows a parked page, so it may be for sale.
6. **The accent is a gift.** The acute on the í is a single pen flick, which makes a natural mark. It also says, in
   the name itself, that this tool writes accents properly, which is the next big feature.
7. **It sits beside Rueca.** Two old craft words, a spinning wheel and a nib, without reusing either.
8. **It doesn't sound like AI.** Seedhand is the safer legal choice, but "seed" is the word people learned from AI
   image tools, and the first thing this product has to say is that it isn't one.

**Risks to accept.** Some English speakers will say PLUM-in at first. The accent disappears in web addresses and
handles (`plumin`). Pen enthusiasts may think of Pilot's Plumix pens. If any of these bother you, take Seedhand.

### 2.5 How it works

1. **Meet a hand.** Each hand is a fictional writer, made from a short code called a seed. The same seed always
   brings back the same writer. Press New hand to meet someone else.
2. **Type.** They write as you type. Each letter is built from a traced skeleton, then shaped by the writer's habits
   and drawn by a simulated pen, so no two come out alike.
3. **Adjust.** Neatness, slant, pen, pressure and ink colour. Small moves, like the difference between your Sunday
   letter and your shopping list.
4. **Keep it.** Copy a link that reopens this exact hand and these settings, download the page as an image, or export
   a font file for other apps.

### 2.6 What you can do with it

- **Thank-you notes, cards and letters** that look written, for when you can't write them all by hand.
- **Props for film, theatre and games:** a diary, a ledger, a letter left on a table. A link brings back the same
  writer for every scene, so the character's hand stays consistent.
- **Labels, menus, place cards and packaging** with a hand that stays the same across the whole set.
- **Journals and templates** set in a hand that feels like a person's.
- **Brand work:** a house hand you can return to by its seed, with a source you can name.
- **Teaching handwriting history:** put an English roundhand next to a German Kurrent and an Italian chancery hand,
  and see how schooling shaped a letter. [after traced hands]
- **Type and design study:** watch what changes when one writer gets neater, faster or switches pens.

### 2.7 Where the hands come from [after traced hands]

Handwriting is learned. How you make a g depends on where and when you went to school, so every hand here starts
from the model someone was taught, then picks up the habits of a real person.

The models come from writing masters whose copybooks are now in the public domain: Ludovico degli Arrighi's
chancery italic (Rome, 1522), Maria Strick's Dutch running hand (1618), George Bickham's *The Universal Penman*
(London, 1733–41), Servidori's Spanish plates (Madrid, 1789), Steensballe's Norwegian roundhand (1849),
Prószyński's Polish school hand (1906) and the Palmer Method (1915), among others.

The habits come from ordinary people who wrote for themselves: wills written by 146 French soldiers in the First
World War, and pages copied out by 267 volunteers for Québec's national library in 2022–24. Both collections are
shared under an open licence. We take the shapes of their letters and leave their words alone.

Where a source isn't free to use, we leave it out. Every skeleton carries its source, and all of them are listed in
the open reference library: [link].

_Today's version:_ The two hands live today are built from Dr. A. V. Hershey's stroke drawings, made at the US
National Bureau of Standards in 1967 and free for any use. Hands traced from five centuries of copybooks and from
ordinary people's writing are being added now. Everything we use or decided against is listed in the open reference
library: [link].

### 2.8 What it isn't

**There's no AI model inside.** Nearly every handwriting dataset is licensed for research only, and we won't train
on handwriting people never agreed to share. Plumín builds each letter with a procedural engine: a skeleton traced
from a credited source, a writer's habits, and a simulated pen and ink. If we ever train a model, it will be on
handwriting people chose to contribute.

**The font file has limits.** A font can carry only a few versions of each letter. Ours carries three and rotates
them, and some apps, Word among them, switch that rotation off unless you turn it on. So the file repeats in a way
the page never does. When it matters, use the page.

**It can't write like you yet,** and it isn't for signatures. A signature should come from your own hand.

### 2.9 FAQ

**Is this AI?**
No. Each letter is built from a traced letter skeleton, shaped by the writer's traits and drawn by a simulated pen.
The rules are written by hand and every source is credited.

**Where does the handwriting come from?**
Today, from Dr. A. V. Hershey's 1967 stroke drawings. Next, from public-domain copybooks dating back to 1522 and from
ordinary people's writing shared under open licences. The reference library lists every source and what we decided
about it.

**Will I see the same letter twice?**
Not on the page. Every letter is drawn fresh, so even a page of 1,500 letters has no exact repeats. The font file is
different: it cycles three versions of each letter.

**How do I keep a hand I like?**
Copy the link. It holds the seed and your settings, and it brings back the same writer every time.

**Can I use it in other apps?**
Download the page as an image, or export a font file. In Word, turn on contextual alternates (Font, then Advanced) to
see all three versions of each letter.

**Can I use it for commercial work?**

**Which languages can it write?**
Today, English and 116 characters, including curly quotes, dashes and € £ ¥. Accented letters for European languages
come next. Until a hand learns a character, it shows as a pencil sketch, so you'll always see what's missing.

**Can it write in my handwriting?**
Not yet. We'd like to build that from handwriting people choose to contribute, with their consent.

## Appendix A: Working for each fact

Engine facts were measured on main at `e89dd3b` with Node 24 on an Apple M1 Max, using a read-only script that
imports `engine/index.js`. The script and its output are reproduced in outline below; nothing in `engine/` was
changed.

**1. Possible writers.** `makeHand(seed, style)` draws every trait from `rng('hand:' + seed)`. In `engine/core.js`,
`rng` hashes the string with `xmur3`, whose state is one 32-bit number, then seeds `sfc32` from it. So the traits
can take at most 2³² = 4,294,967,296 distinct sets per style. Check: among 1,000,000 random seeds, 122 shared a
trait set with an earlier seed; a 32-bit space predicts 116.4 (n²/2³³). Among 2,000,000 seeds: 480 vs 465.7.
Two styles × 2³² = 8,589,934,592. A writer's quirk field comes from a second hash (`makeNoise('hand:' + seed)`), and
in both collisions found, the quirks differed, so the true count is higher; I'm quoting the lower bound. World
population: about 8.2 billion in 2024 (UN, *World Population Prospects 2024*). Near-twins: `tools/qa/REPORT.md`
§2, 200 hands per style, nearest-neighbour distance under 1.5 just-noticeable differences.

**2. Traits and controls.** Keys of `makeHand('alpha')` whose numeric values differ from `makeHand('beta')`: 24
(neatness, slant, penWidth, pressure, nibAngle, xHeight, ascender, descender, width, capWidth, roundness,
sidebearing, space, strokeShift, wordSpace, quirk, quirkScale, tremor, drift, pressureVar, taperIn, taperOut,
dotHeight, dotSize). `PARAMS` in `engine/index.js` exposes 5 keys: neatness, slant, pen, penWidth, pressure.

**3. Identical letters.** Text: four pangrams repeated 12 times, laid out with `layout(p, text, { maxWidth: 14000 })`.
1,572 glyphs over 32 distinct characters; same-letter pairs = Σ n(n−1)/2 = 47,094. Compared each glyph's full
outline (`JSON.stringify(g.contours)`): 0 duplicates in print and in script. Why: each use gets its own random stream
(`'inst:' + seed + ':live:' + position + ':' + ch`), and each stroke's bow and offset use further streams. An exact
repeat needs at least two independent 32-bit draws to coincide, about 1 in 1.8 × 10¹⁹ per pair. (A lone dot, like a
full stop, uses one stream: 1 in 4.29 billion.) Closest pairs: `tools/qa/REPORT.md` §5, rubric row 1.

**4. Characters.** Scanned U+0020 to U+2FFF with `has(p, ch)`: 116 in each style, 95 ASCII plus 21 extras
(` ¢£¥©®°·×÷–—‘’“”•…€™−` and the non-breaking space). Target 324 = GF Latin Core (`docs/character-set.md`).

**5. Points per letter.** 12 seeds × 26 lowercase letters × 4 uses = 1,248 instances per style, via
`makeGlyph(p, ch, { seed, amount: 1 })`. Means: print 2.00 strokes, 189.3 centreline points, 406.7 outline points;
script 2.31 strokes, 271.2 centreline points, 574.7 outline points. Joined g: 351.2 centreline, 730.3 outline.
The product UI session measured about 550 outline points per letter independently (side-session notes).

**6. Speed.** A 576-character note (456 letters), 5 warm-up runs, then the median of 25 runs of `layout` +
`renderSVG`: print 91.1 ms (52.5 + 37.5), 2.2 MB of SVG; script 112.3 ms (58.4 + 51.7), 3.0 MB. 456 / 0.0911 s =
5,007 letters/s; 456 / 0.1123 s = 4,061 letters/s. Human pace: 2.5–3 letters/s, the real-time speed of the write-on
animation on branch `claude/interesting-shaw-e463ac` (side-session notes). 4,061 / 3 = 1,354, so "more than 1,300
times". The QA harness measured 89–104 ms (print) and 113–133 ms (script) per 600 characters on the same machine.

**7. Font versions.** `buildFont` defaults to `alts: 3` and cycles them with `calt` (`engine/fontbuild.js`).
