## Summary (round 1, superseded by the decision above)

- **Recommended name: Plumín** (ploo-MEEN). In Spanish it's the nib of a fountain pen, the part that touches the
  paper. It reads as "pen" across the Romance languages, has no trademark in software in the US register or in
  TMview, and `plumin.ink` is free.
- **Next best:** **Seedhand** (the cleanest legally, already in the code, but "seed" is AI-image vocabulary),
  **Penfolk** (warm and people-first, zero marks), **Everyhand** (says "inclusive" in one word, zero marks).
- **Ruled out after checking:** Fair Hand (a kids' handwriting app already uses it), Longhand (an app for
  handwritten notes plus live marks for direct mail and software), Otherhand (Cheetos released "The Other Hand"
  font), Tintero (a writing-studio app), Ductus and Rasgo (live marks in software classes), Penfriend (a typeface
  and an AI writer).
- **Strongest facts:** 8,589,934,592 possible writers (more than the 8.2 billion people alive); 0 identical
  letters in 47,094 chances on one page; sources spanning 1522 to 2024; about 4,000 to 5,000 letters drawn per
  second; 484 open references.
- **Two claims need to wait.** "Five centuries of handwriting" and "billions of *different-looking* hands" are true
  of the research and the maths, but the engine today still draws from one 1967 source and many hands look like
  siblings. Both change when the traced traditions and letter variety land. Until then, use the "today" versions
  marked below.

## 1. Naming

### 1.1 What the name has to do

1. Feel warm and made by hand, like the product.
2. Point at writing: a pen, a hand, paper, a writer.
3. Work across languages, since the hands come from many countries.
4. Be clear of existing handwriting apps, fonts and software trademarks.
5. Avoid sounding like AI. The product's first promise is that there's no AI model inside.
6. Have a domain path: an exact `.com` is rare now, so `.ink`, `.co`, `.app` or a short prefix count.
7. Respect Rafael's other names (Rueca, the mini series, Sombra, Trama, Matiz, Molde, Vaivén) without reusing them.

### 1.5 Recommendation: Plumín

1. **It names the exact spot where handwriting happens.** The nib is where ink leaves the pen. Every letter this
   tool draws is a simulated nib moving across paper.
2. **It's warm and small,** like the product's best moments: a note, a label, a letter.
3. **It travels.** Spanish, French, Catalan and Portuguese speakers hear "pen" or "feather" in it; English and Dutch
   speakers hear "plume". It's easy to say once you've heard it.
4. **It's clear where it matters.** No mark in software in the US register or TMview; the exact marks that exist
   sit in lighting, clothing and cosmetics.
5. **`plumin.ink` is free,** a domain that says ink. `plumin.com` shows a parked page, so it may be for sale.
6. **The accent is a gift.** The acute on the í is a single pen flick, which makes a natural mark. It also says, in

7. **It sits beside Rueca.** Two old craft words, a spinning wheel and a nib, without reusing either.
8. **It doesn't sound like AI.** Seedhand is the safer legal choice, but "seed" is the word people learned from AI

### 2.4 A hundred and fifty words

People wrote these letters to someone they loved, missed or worried about. A child asking the Three Kings for a

### 2.5 How it works

1. **Open a letter.** Every letter here was written by a real person, mostly ordinary people from 1395 to 1929
   **[count]**. Shuffle to find another.
2. **Watch it written.** A fictional writer copies it out on letter paper, stroke by stroke. Each writer has their own
   hand, pen and habits. Press New hand to meet someone else.
3. **Write back.** Start typing. Your reply goes down in ink on a fresh sheet, with the found letter tucked
   underneath. Deleting crosses a word out; a retyped letter writes over the old one.
4. **Send it on.** Share a link and the recipient watches your letter being written. Or download the page, or the
   handwriting as a font.

### 2.6 Where the letters come from

Most letters that survive were written by the famous. These weren't. They're letters by emigrants, children, sailors'
sweethearts, settlers, farmers and mothers, kept by families, seized in wartime, or never delivered, and later
printed or archived. Most of them are by women **[count: 59%]**.

Among them: Argentine children writing to the Three Kings, Liberian settlers writing home, families on the move
during the Great Migration of 1917, a Tejana in Béxar in 1824, and letters in fourteen languages **[count]**. A few
famous voices are here too, like Phillis Wheatley and Ignatius Sancho.

Every letter is in the public domain, and every one carries its source. If a source doesn't say something, neither
do we.

### 2.7 Where the hands come from

Handwriting is learned. How you make a g depends on where and when you went to school, so each fictional writer
starts from a model someone was taught, then picks up the habits of a real person.

The models come from public-domain copybooks: Ludovico degli Arrighi's chancery italic (Rome, 1522), Maria Strick's
Dutch running hand (1618), George Bickham's *The Universal Penman* (London, 1733–41), Servidori's Spanish plates
(Madrid, 1789) and the Palmer Method (1915), among others. The habits come from ordinary people who shared their
writing under open licences, from French soldiers' wills of the First World War to volunteers in Québec in 2022–24.
We take the shapes of their letters and leave their words alone. Every source is listed in the open reference
library.

_Use this until the traced hands ship:_ The hands live today are built from Dr. A. V. Hershey's 1967 stroke drawings.
Hands traced from five centuries of copybooks are being added now.

### 2.8 What it is, plainly

**A lab piece.** It's free, it isn't a business, and it's an experiment in what can be built now that couldn't be
before: hundreds of believable hands nobody would have drawn one by one.

**How the handwriting is made.** A procedural engine builds every letter from a traced skeleton, a writer's habits
and a simulated pen and ink. It isn't a model trained on people's handwriting, and it uses no scraped handwriting.
AI helped build the engine and research the sources. **[NEED: Rafael, confirm you want the AI-assisted build
mentioned here; it matches "using AI in ways I think is clever".]**

**What the fonts can't do.** A font file can only carry a few versions of each letter. Ours carries three and
rotates them, and some apps switch that rotation off. So a downloaded font repeats in a way the page never does.

**Whose hand it is.** The handwriting is never the original writer's. A fictional hand writes each letter out.

## Appendix A: Working for each fact

Engine facts were measured on main at `e89dd3b` with Node 24 on an Apple M1 Max, using a read-only script that
imports `engine/index.js`. The script and its output are reproduced in outline below; nothing in `engine/` was
changed.

**1. Possible writers.** `makeHand(seed, style)` draws every trait from `rng('hand:' + seed)`. In `engine/core.js`,
`rng` hashes the string with `xmur3`, whose state is one 32-bit number, then seeds `sfc32` from it. So the traits
can take at most 2³² = 4,294,967,296 distinct sets per style. Check: among 1,000,000 random seeds, 122 shared a
trait set with an earlier seed; a 32-bit space predicts 116.4 (n²/2³³). Among 2,000,000 seeds: 480 vs 465.7.
Two styles × 2³² = 8,589,934,592. A writer's quirk field comes from a second hash (`makeNoise('hand:' + seed)`), and
in both collisions found, the quirks differed, so the true count is higher; I'm quoting the lower bound. World
population: about 8.2 billion in 2024 (UN, *World Population Prospects 2024*). Near-twins: `tools/qa/REPORT.md`
§2, 200 hands per style, nearest-neighbour distance under 1.5 just-noticeable differences.

**2. Traits and controls.** Keys of `makeHand('alpha')` whose numeric values differ from `makeHand('beta')`: 24
(neatness, slant, penWidth, pressure, nibAngle, xHeight, ascender, descender, width, capWidth, roundness,
sidebearing, space, strokeShift, wordSpace, quirk, quirkScale, tremor, drift, pressureVar, taperIn, taperOut,
dotHeight, dotSize). `PARAMS` in `engine/index.js` exposes 5 keys: neatness, slant, pen, penWidth, pressure.

**3. Identical letters.** Text: four pangrams repeated 12 times, laid out with `layout(p, text, { maxWidth: 14000 })`.
1,572 glyphs over 32 distinct characters; same-letter pairs = Σ n(n−1)/2 = 47,094. Compared each glyph's full
outline (`JSON.stringify(g.contours)`): 0 duplicates in print and in script. Why: each use gets its own random stream
(`'inst:' + seed + ':live:' + position + ':' + ch`), and each stroke's bow and offset use further streams. An exact
repeat needs at least two independent 32-bit draws to coincide, about 1 in 1.8 × 10¹⁹ per pair. (A lone dot, like a
full stop, uses one stream: 1 in 4.29 billion.) Closest pairs: `tools/qa/REPORT.md` §5, rubric row 1.
