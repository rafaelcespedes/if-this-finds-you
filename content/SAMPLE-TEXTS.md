# Lost Letters: the sample letters

Before anyone types, each new hand writes a real letter. Under the working title **Lost Letters**, the collection is mostly
letters by **ordinary people**: undelivered, seized, emigrant, family and everyday letters, each with its true origin.
Nothing is invented. If a source doesn't say it, we don't say it.

- `sample-texts.json`: the collection, in the order "New hand" steps through it. The app shows only `enabled` items.
- `check-texts.mjs`: run `node content/check-texts.mjs` to validate it (add `--list` to print every enabled item).
- This file: the targets, where the letters come from, the rights rules, what failed and why, the credits, and the open
  questions.

## At a glance

- **127 letters switched on, out of 223 in the file.** The 96 that are off are mostly first-round letters by famous
  writers, kept as a checked reserve (see "Switched off").
- **Ordinary people: 106 of 127 (83%).** The other 21 are by famous writers.
- **Women: 75 of 127 (59%).** Men 39; 13 letters whose source doesn't state the writer's sex (children signing with
  initials, joint husband-and-wife letters, unsigned notes). Among the ordinary letters: 62 women, 31 men.
- **African and African American writers: 25.** Liberian settlers, free and freed African Americans before 1865, Great
  Migration letters of 1917, plus Sancho, Wheatley, Banneker, Dunbar and Machado de Assis.
- **Hispanic and Latin American writers: 21.** Argentine children writing to the Three Kings, a boy in Ciudad Real, a
  Tejana in 1824 Béxar, Cuban, Mexican, Spanish and Puerto Rican children, a Spanish duchess writing to her son, plus Goya,
  Sor Juana, Teresa of Ávila, Avellaneda, Gorriti and Machado de Assis.
- **Where they come from:** 35 family, 31 everyday, 29 emigrant, 7 undelivered, 4 seized, and 21 by famous writers.
- **Languages:** English 75 (59%), Spanish 13, French 8, German 6, Dutch 5, Czech 5, Italian 4, Danish 3, Welsh 2,
  Norwegian 2, and Swedish, Hungarian, Romanian and Portuguese 1 each.
- **Span:** 1395 to 1929. The 28 written before 1700 are marked `olderHandsOnly`.
- **123 different writers.** 122 letters and 5 notes, 16 to 89 words (median 77).

**The `writer` field.** `writer: { gender?, heritage?, basis? }` is filled only where the source states it plainly, and
`basis` quotes or summarises what the source says ("a colored female, who sailed from this city about four years since";
"I am a little Cuban girl"). Nothing is guessed from a name. Where the source is silent, the field is left out. That is
why "Barbary Stockdell", "Will T. Polk", "Maybelle" and "Helen" have no gender, and why 13 letters have none.

**How the groups are counted.** The checker reads `writer.heritage`:
- **African or African American:** African American, Afro-British, Afro-Brazilian, Liberian settler, Sierra Leone settler,
  "of colour" and similar.
- **Hispanic or Latin American:** Spanish, Mexican, Cuban, Puerto Rican, Argentine, Venezuelan, Brazilian, Tejano and
  similar. A letter **written in Spanish** (or Brazilian Portuguese) also counts, because Hispanic means Spanish-speaking.
  That covers, for example, the Argentine children and the Tejana María Josefa Becerra, whose sources give an address
  but no nationality.

## The idea

Rafael's brief (2 November 2026): an archive of letters by ordinary people, mostly undelivered or everyday, each with its
true origin, never invented; keep only a handful of famous letters. Then: no permission requests to any institution
(use only sources that already pass), 20 famous letters is fine, and inclusivity is a hard target: "include a lot of
women, include a lot of African-Americans or Africans, and definitely include a lot of Hispanics."

**Ordinary** means the writer is not famous: someone known only because their letter survived. Settlers, farmers,
emigrants, teachers, merchants' wives, children and servants count. So do relatives of famous people who weren't famous
themselves (Goethe's wife and son; Amelia Loguen, a bishop's daughter; the Huygens sisters).

## Rights rules

Every enabled letter must be public domain in **both** the US and the EU. The checker re-derives `rights.us` and `rights.eu`
from the recorded facts and fails on any mismatch.

**US: passes if either**
- **U1:** first printed or published **before 1931** (`rights.firstPublished`). This covers 125 letters, including foreign
  newspapers of 1923–24, whose 95-year US terms have run out; or
- **U2:** never published before **2003**, and the writer died before 1956 (or, if the death year is unknown, the letter
  was written before 1906). Under 17 U.S.C. §302 and §303(a), unpublished works run life + 70, or 120 years from creation.
  Two letters use this path: the 1744 Prize Papers letter (The National Archives, 2024) and the 1706 Brienne note (2026).
- **Fails:** first published between **1931 and 2002**. Old letters first published from 1978 to 2002 stay protected in
  the US until at least 2047. That rules out, for example, *Slaves No More* (1980), *Dear Master* (1978), *Our Children Free
  and Happy* (1991), Otte's Spanish emigrants (1988), Davis's Paston letters (1971–76) and Mariquita Sánchez (1952).

**EU and UK: passes if either**
- **E1:** the writer died before 1956, or was certainly dead by then. `authorDiedBy` holds an upper bound, usually the
  latest likely birth year plus 110 to 120 years (an adult settler writing from Liberia in 1834 was dead by the 1930s).
  This covers 98 letters.
- **E2:** published **anonymously** before 1956 (`rights.anonymous: true`; 70 years from publication). This covers 29
  letters: children signing with a first name or initials, letters printed with names cut (Hyde, the 1919 Great Migration
  letters), and settlers printed without names.
- **The EU publication right:** a letter first made public in 2001 or later must come from an **openly licensed** source
  (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA, Public Domain Mark, Open Government Licence). NC and ND licences fail. The checker enforces this.

**No translations** among the enabled letters: every letter is in the language it was written in.

## Judgement calls worth knowing

- **Anonymity:** a child signing "Maybelle", "Pochi Mari Elena" or "Antonio G." is treated as unidentifiable. Three of the
  Argentine girls printed street addresses next to their first names. We cut the addresses; the rights note says so.
- **Liberian settlers:** *The African Repository* printed these letters as letters from colonists in Liberia, the colony
  the American Colonization Society founded for free African Americans. For most writers it says so plainly ("a colored
  female"). For Elizabeth Winder and Will T. Polk it doesn't state their colour individually, and their heritage is
  recorded only as "Liberian settler".
- **Letters to former owners or patrons:** Rosabella Burke writes to Mary Custis Lee, Mary Jackson to John McDonogh, and
  Eunice Moore to her New York supporters. The excerpts keep to children, farms and school. Ellen Saunders's story says
  plainly that she had escaped slavery, because that is where her letter comes from.
- **Ordinary or not:** Amelia Loguen (a bishop's daughter, later Frederick Douglass's daughter-in-law), the Duquesa de
  Villahermosa (an aristocrat, not famous), Christiane Vulpius and Marjory Fleming are counted as ordinary. David George, a
  well-known Black Baptist preacher, is counted as famous and kept in reserve.
- **The Egypt letters of 1798:** tender letters home from soldiers and officials, seized at sea. The setting is war.
- **Labels on old texts:** some scans carry later non-commercial labels from their uploaders (Livi's 1910 Datini book, the
  Bavarian State Library's images of an 1839 paper). We rely on the public-domain printings and our own transcriptions.
- **Van Gogh:** both letters stay off until they are re-checked against the 1914 printing.

## How the text is written

- **The writer's own spelling,** including Middle English, 17th-century Dutch and Czech, Liselotte's 1659 German, and the
  unschooled spelling of many settlers and migrants ("i recived you bind leter"). Long s (ſ) becomes s, straight quotes
  become curly, and editors' glosses and footnote marks are left out. The abbreviation "Dⁿ" (superscript n) in a
  Tejano letter is written "Don", because the font set has no superscript n.
- **Older hands only:** letters written before 1700 carry `olderHandsOnly: true` and pair only with pre-1700 traditions.
- **Cuts:** every cut is marked with "…" (Rafael: "Keep '…' for cuts"). Inside a paragraph it sits between the two
  parts (" … "). If the rest of a paragraph is cut, it closes that paragraph ("with profound emotion. …"); if the start
  of a paragraph is cut, it opens it ("… The hills take off"). A writer's own dots stay as full stops.
- **No invented greetings or signatures.**
- **Undated letters** carry the year they were printed and a `dateNote`.

## Layout

Each letter keeps the paragraph and line breaks of its source. The layout pass went back to every enabled letter's
printed page (page images, Gutenberg texts, Wikisource) and changed **only line breaks**: a script refused any change to
the letters themselves.

- **69 of 127 letters now have paragraph breaks** (before: none). Up to 7 paragraphs; 22 letters have 3 or more.
- **`layout: "source"` for 100 letters:** the breaks follow the print, including letters that really are one paragraph
  between a greeting and a closing printed on their own lines.
- **`layout: "inferred"` for 27 letters:** the source runs the letter on (common in newspapers and unschooled writing),
  so the body stays one paragraph and only the greeting, the closing and, in two letters, a P.S. are set apart.
- Greetings are on their own line; multi-line closings keep their lines ("Your moste infenitt / affectionat Mother /
  I. W."); a P.S. or N.B. is its own paragraph; datelines at the top stay on their own line.

**The "…" pass.** The layout pass found 67 cuts in 48 letters that weren't marked: mostly cuts at a paragraph edge, which
the earlier convention left unmarked, and two places where a line break hid a cut (Christiane Vulpius's letter). All are
now marked as described under "How the text is written". A script confirmed the only change was "…" plus the space next
to it.

**Small corrections confirmed against the print:** "My dear Child,—" (the Christmas card), "Mé přenejmilejší dítě! —"
(Zuzana Černínová), "MAYBELLE." as printed, "I am yours——." with its full stop, and "before you left home. and" (John
Miller Jr). Two source references were corrected (Julien François's pages, Kateřina L.'s page link). Left as is: the
1877 print splits "Alle-sandra" across a line end, so "Alessandra" stays; the Tejano "Dⁿ Santiago" stays "Don" because
the font set has no superscript n.

## Order

The array order is the "New hand" order. It opens with Maybelle's 1908 Santa letter, Rosabella Burke's "My children are as
fat as pigs" from Liberia, Pochi's letter to the Three Kings from Buenos Aires, a letter seized from Egypt, a Cuban girl's
Angora cat, Martha Snetter's news for her mother from Monrovia, a Sussex emigrant's new baby, a Tejana asking for two good
hoes, a Danish widow's village news, a love letter with wedding cake from 1850, Dickinson's "Dear Peacock" and a notary
borrowing a melon. After that, four streams are woven evenly: African and African American, Hispanic and Latin American,
other English, and other languages. Each stream rotates through the origins. The same language never comes more than
four times in a row, and two letters by the same writer are at least ten apart. Switched-off letters come last.

## Characters

`check-texts.mjs` imports GF Latin Core from `tools/qa/lib/charsets.mjs` (315 characters) and falls back to the same
derivation. Every enabled letter passes. Things avoided on purpose: the yogh (ȝ), Welsh ŵ and ŷ, and superscript
abbreviations (cut, skipped or expanded). The Vietnamese item stays off until Tier 3.

## Switched off (96)

- **87 famous writers' letters** retired from the default set but kept, rights-checked and sourced. They include Austen,
  Twain, Mozart, Verdi, Chopin, Keats, Steele, George Sand, Rosa Luxemburg and Frederick Douglass. Any can come back while
  the targets hold.
- **4 English everyday letters** retired to keep the balance (a pig for sale, mithridate mustard, Walter Paston's Oxford
  feast, William Cooper's dog).
- **David George, 1793** (Sierra Leone): counted as famous, kept in reserve.
- **Two Van Gogh letters:** held until re-checked against the 1914 printing.
- **Jean François Noel, 1812:** held for Rafael. A French prisoner of war's never-sent letter, found under a floor in 1881.
- **Vietnamese:** off until Tier 3; a travel journal, not a letter.

## Open questions for Rafael

1. **The 1798 Egypt letters** (3 on): tender letters from Bonaparte's army, seized by Nelson. Keep them on?
2. **The 1812 prisoner's never-sent letter:** switch it on?
3. **Spanish-language letters count as Hispanic** even when the source gives no nationality. Agree?
4. **Hispanic depth:** the Hispanic letters are mostly children's (Three Kings, children's magazines) plus one Tejana, one
   duchess and the famous six. More adult everyday letters probably sit behind the blocked Spanish and US newspaper
   archives. Worth a person spending an hour browsing them?
