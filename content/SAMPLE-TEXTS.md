# Lost Letters: the sample letters

Before anyone types, each new hand writes a real letter. Under the working title **Lost Letters**, the collection is mostly
letters by **ordinary people**: undelivered, seized, emigrant, family and everyday letters, each with its true origin.
Nothing is invented. If a source doesn't say it, we don't say it.

- `sample-texts.json`: the collection, in the order "New hand" steps through it. The app shows only `enabled` items.
- `check-texts.mjs`: run `node content/check-texts.mjs` to validate it (add `--list` to print every enabled item).
- This file: where the letters come from, the rights rules, what was looked at and failed, the credits, and the open
  questions.

## At a glance

- **95 letters switched on, out of 185 in the file.** The 90 switched off are mostly first-round letters by famous
  writers, kept as a checked reserve (see "Switched off").
- **Ordinary people: 75 of 95 (79%).** The checker fails below 70%.
- **Where they come from (enabled):** 32 family, 18 emigrant, 14 everyday, 7 undelivered, 4 seized, and 20 by famous writers.
- **Writers:** 91 people. Of the 95 letters, 53 are by women, 39 by men and 3 by unknown writers. Among the 75 ordinary
  letters: 43 women, 29 men, 3 unknown. Many are by children: Marjory Fleming aged 8, August von Goethe aged 7, a Verney
  grandson, five 1908 letters to Santa and three St. Nicholas readers.
- **Languages (enabled):** English 52 (55%), French 8, German 6, Dutch 5, Czech 5, Italian 4, Danish 3, Spanish 3, Welsh 2,
  Norwegian 2, Polish, Hungarian, Swedish, Romanian and Portuguese 1 each.
- **Span:** 1395 to 1914. 28 letters are from before 1700 and are marked `olderHandsOnly`.
- **Kinds:** 88 letters and 7 notes. Length 16 to 89 words, median 76.

## The idea

Rafael's brief (2 November 2026): an archive of letters by ordinary people, mostly undelivered or everyday, each with its
true origin, never invented. Keep only a handful of famous letters, the best ones. At least 70% of the switched-on letters
must be by ordinary, non-famous people. Same rules as before: public domain in both the US and the EU, Latin script, 15 to
90 words, warm in tone, and no war or death letters as defaults.

**Ordinary** means the writer is not famous: someone known only because their letter survived. Sailors' and soldiers'
sweethearts, farmers, emigrants, merchants' wives, children and servants count. So do the relatives of famous people who
weren't famous themselves (Galileo's daughter is the exception: her letter stays with the famous ones). The `origin` field
records this: everything except `notable` counts as ordinary.

## Rights rules

Every enabled letter must be public domain in **both** the US and the EU. The checker re-derives `rights.us` and `rights.eu`
from the recorded facts and fails on any mismatch.

**US: passes if either**
- **U1:** first printed or published **before 1931** (`rights.firstPublished`); 93 letters, or
- **U2:** never published before **2003** (first made public in 2003 or later), and the writer died before 1956. If the
  death year is unknown, the letter must be written before 1906. Under 17 U.S.C. §302 and §303(a), unpublished works run
  life + 70, or 120 years from creation. Two letters use this path: the 1744 Prize Papers letter (published by The
  National Archives in 2024) and the 1706 Brienne note (published in 2026).
- **Fails:** first published between **1931 and 2002**. Old letters first published from 1978 to 2002 stay protected in
  the US until at least 2047, however old they are. This rule rules out several well-known modern editions (see below).

**EU and UK: passes if either**
- **E1:** the writer died before 1956, or was certainly dead by then. `rights.authorDied` holds the exact year, or
  `rights.authorDiedBy` holds an upper bound with the reasoning in `basis`, usually the latest likely birth year plus 110 to
  120 years. Example: an adult emigrant writing in 1832 died by 1930 at the latest. 77 letters pass this way.
- **E2:** published **anonymously** before 1956 (`rights.anonymous: true`). The Term Directive gives anonymous works 70
  years from publication. 17 letters are anonymous: the Santa letters, the St. Nicholas letters, the letters printed by
  Hyde with names blanked, and three emigrants printed under a first name or initials. For 16 of them this is the
  path that passes.
- **The EU publication right:** whoever first publishes an old, unpublished work gets 25 years of rights. So any letter
  first made public in 2001 or later must come from a source with an **open licence** (CC0, CC BY, CC BY-SA, Public Domain
  Mark, Open Government Licence). The checker enforces this from `source.licence`. NC and ND licences fail.

**Translations:** none among the enabled letters. Every letter is in the language it was written in.

**Edge cases:**
- **Anonymity:** a child signing "Grace C." or "Maybelle" in a 1908 newspaper is treated as anonymous, since the writer
  can't be identified. This is low risk, but it is a judgement.
- **The Egypt letters of 1798:** soldiers and officials in a military campaign. The excerpts are only about love and home
  ("paradise, for me, is my house near Toulon"), but the setting is war. See the open questions.
- **Margrethe Pedersen** (Danish, 1892): her letters were privately printed for the family around 1915. If that doesn't count
  as publication, the letter still passes U2 (she died in 1902), and the Royal Danish Library releases the text as public
  domain.
- **Mismatched labels on old texts:** Ser Lapo Mazzei's 1398 letter comes from a 1910 printing whose Internet Archive scan
  carries a later CC BY-NC-SA label from the uploader. We rely on the 1910 printing. Similarly, DBNL marks Worp's 1911
  Huygens edition "possibly protected", but the editor died in 1922. The Bavarian State Library's images of the 1839 German
  letter are non-commercial; we use our own transcription of the 1839 text, not the images.
- **Van Gogh:** both letters stay switched off until they are re-checked against the 1914 printing (decision of 2 Nov).

## How the text is written

- **The writer's own spelling,** including Middle English ("my ryght welebeloved Voluntyne"), Dutch "myn", old Czech and
  Liselotte's 1659 German. Nothing is modernised. Long s (ſ) becomes s; straight quotes become curly; editors' glosses and
  footnote marks are left out.
- **Older hands only:** letters written before 1700 carry `olderHandsOnly: true` and pair only with the pre-1700
  traditions (chancery italic, Dutch running hand, Kurrent). This follows the decision that very old spellings go only with
  older-looking hands.
- **Cuts:** "…" marks a cut inside a sentence or paragraph (decision: keep them). Cuts at a sentence or paragraph break,
  or around the greeting and sign-off, aren't marked. A writer's own dots stay as full stops.
- **No invented greetings or signatures.** Some sources print letters without them (decision: keep those letters).
- **Undated letters** (Hyde's, printed 1889) carry `year: 1889` and a `dateNote` saying they were written by then.

## Fields

```
id              lowercase kebab-case (e.g. "santa-1908-iceland-money")
text            the letter, with \n line breaks
kind            letter | note | postcard
lang            BCP-47 (en-GB, en-US, fr, nl, cs, da, no, sv, cy, ...)
title           short
author          the name as printed; unsigned letters say so ("Unknown (signed 'C. B.')")
recipient       who it was written to, where known
place           where it was written, where known
year            the year written (dateNote when only a "written by" year is known)
origin          undelivered | seized | family | emigrant | everyday | notable        (required)
story           one true sentence about where the letter came from                    (required)
source          { name, url, licence?, licenceUrl? }   licence is required for anything first published in 2001 or later
rights          { us, eu, basis, firstPublished (year, or null if never published),
                  authorDied | authorDiedBy (upper bound), anonymous? }
translator      { name, died, published }   (none enabled)
tags            content tags (family, love, food, weather, children, animals, money, travel, ...)
traditions      hand styles it pairs with (provisional ids, docs/research/hand-traditions.md §8)
olderHandsOnly  true for letters written before 1700
enabled         false = not shown by default
disabledReason  why it is switched off
```

## Order

The array order is the "New hand" order. It opens with a Santa letter (Maybelle ordering Papa's cap), a seized letter from
Egypt, an emigrant mother's new baby, a Danish widow's village news measured in cows, Dickinson's "Dear Peacock" and a
notary borrowing a melon. After that the origins take turns in proportion to their size, the same language rarely comes
twice in a row, and letters by the same writer are spread apart. Switched-off letters come last.

## Characters

`check-texts.mjs` imports GF Latin Core from `tools/qa/lib/charsets.mjs` (315 characters). It falls back to the same
derivation if that file moves. Every enabled letter passes. Things avoided on purpose: the yogh (ȝ) in some Middle English
editions, Welsh ŵ and ŷ, and superscript abbreviations (cut or skipped). The Vietnamese item stays off until Tier 3.

## Switched off

- **86 famous writers' letters from the first round** are retired from the default set but kept, rights-checked and
  sourced. They include Austen, Twain, Mozart, Verdi, Van Gogh, George Sand and Rosa Luxemburg. Any of them can be switched
  back on, as long as the ordinary share stays at 70% or more.
- **Two Van Gogh letters** are held until they are re-checked against the 1914 printing.
- **Jean François Noel, 1812** is held for Rafael. He was a French prisoner of war near Edinburgh, and his letter was never
  sent; it was found under a floor in 1881. The text is about longing for news, but the setting is a prison camp.
- **Vietnamese** (Trương Vĩnh Ký, 1876) is off: it needs Tier 3, and it is a travel journal, not a letter.

## Left out on tone or form (round 2)

- **Tone:** cholera deaths in the Sussex letters; Lydia Hilton's baby's death (her other letter is used); the 1757 Brienne
  letter asking for a death certificate; most of the 1798 Egypt letters (marching, plague, battle); the 1915 *Maternity:
  Letters from Working-Women* (rights pass; the subject is childbirth hardship); Isabel de Guevara's letter of 1556 (hunger
  in early Buenos Aires).
- **Form:** Swedish Emigration Commission replies that are long essays; the 1709 German "emigrant letters" (petitions to a
  prince); Iorga's Romanian merchant letters (summaries only); newspaper "letters" that turned out to be fiction.
- **Not ordinary:** Lucy Larcom, Mary Leadbeater, Marie de l'Incarnation, Padre Isla.
- **Named children with unknown death dates** (St. Nicholas letters signed with full names, Jerrine Rupert born 1906): they
  fail the EU test unless printed anonymously.

Round-1 decisions and rejections (Mistral, Ibarbourou, Keller, Twain's Santa letter, Darío, Martí and others) are listed
in the round-1 notes in this file's git history (commit before the Lost Letters shift).

## Open questions for Rafael

1. **Ask for permission to unlock the big collections?** Three letters would do most: the Instituut voor de Nederlandse
   Taal (Letters as Loot, Gekaapte Brieven), Sound & Vision (the Brienne images), and the Prize Papers Project. Each could
   add hundreds of seized or undelivered letters by ordinary women and men.
2. **The 1798 Egypt letters** (3 enabled): tender letters home from Bonaparte's army, seized by Nelson. Keep them on?
3. **The 1812 prisoner's never-sent letter:** switch it on?
4. **Twenty famous letters kept,** four of them for language spread (Portuguese, Spanish, Hungarian, Romanian), because
   no ordinary letters in those languages passed. Fewer?
5. **Gaps:** no ordinary letters passed in Spanish, Portuguese, Hungarian, Romanian or Irish. None of the ordinary letters
   are by writers of colour; the candidates (a free Black woman's 1793 seized letter, for example) exist only in paraphrase
   or under closed licences. A targeted search, or the permissions in question 1, would help most here.
