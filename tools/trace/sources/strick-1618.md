## Dutch running hand (lopende hand), 1605–1620s — style `dutch-1618` ("Dutch running hand 1618", joined)

| Item | Maria Strick (1577–after 1631), schoolmistress and writing master in Delft and Rotterdam, *Schat oft voorbeelt ende verthooninge van verscheyden geschriften ten dienste vande liefhebbers der hooch-loflycker konste der penne*, Rotterdam, 1618, engraved by Hans Strick. Sheet 31, "Voorbeelden van de Nederlandse kapitalen, de staande letter en cursieve hand": the "Nederlandtsche Capitalen ofte Voor-letteren" (two rows, A–Z, most letters in two forms) and the "Fondament vande Loopende Letter" exercises (small letters in alphabetical groups). The upright "staende Letter" block on the same sheet was read only to tell letters apart. Jan van de Velde, *Spieghel der Schrijfkonste* (Rotterdam, 1605), plates 20, 21 and 24 (running-hand text) were read for the movement (joins, e, d, h and g in running words), not traced |

| Traced | a–z, long s ſ and the Dutch ĳ (the plate's joined ij); second forms of b (straight stem), d (looped), k (straight stem, knotted arm), l (hooked, unlooped), r (2-shaped r rotunda) and v (closed loop). Small letters are 14–15 px high on the plate, so each was skeletonised from a 5× upsampled crop and the strokes put in writing order by hand (`manual`). A–Z from the capital rows (the pipeline's skeleton, hand-fixed where ruled lines or broken hairlines got in), with second forms of B C D E F G K L N O Q R S T V W; U uses the V capitals (one letter in 1618). Left out: the first M (its hairlines break into five pieces) and the first P (stem and swash cannot be separated); the sheet has no digits or punctuation, which borrow the `script` forms. j is the second limb of the plate's ĳ. Accented letters are left to the accents module |

| Movement | A gothic running hand, nearly upright (about 3°), with a very small x-height under tall ascenders (3–4 x-heights on the plate). It does not run on one join line: `joins: natural`, so every letter keeps the plate's own lead-ins and exits, and they touch where the plate's do. Minims are angular and joined foot-to-head by diagonal hairlines, so n looks like u and m like three strokes (m.2). The e is the secretary (reversed) e laid flat: a long low stroke rising into a small eye at the top right, then a hairline out. c runs out along a long top hairline at the x-height. d leans its ascender far back to the left over a small cup (uncial d), or loops back (∂). h is the secretary h: a hooked ascender stops at the x-height and the limb swings right and below the baseline, sweeping back left under the previous letter; z is a tailed ʒ that drops a round belly below the line; y has a straight hairline tail to the lower left; g is a figure-of-eight; f and ſ hang long descenders from a looped head; s is the round 6-shaped final s; b closes its bowl with a bar across the stem; v closes its bowl back over the top. Capitals are big, broad, flourished forms built on C and O shapes with inner spirals and curls (D is a reversed C with an inner curl, E a C with an inner E), and they do not join |
| Note | x-height ratio 0.30 of the cap (the plate's own proportion; the engine's hand sampler currently sets its own x-height for non-`script` styles, so renders show a larger x-height than the plate).
